01 · Site and rules

What a 100 m² lot lets you build

The plan below assumes a 10 × 10 m lot with one street façade and party walls on the other three sides, in a zone classed for R+2 buildings on a courtyard (the "E2 / habitat économique" type found in most lotissements). Before buying or drawing anything, ask the Agence Urbaine for the note de renseignements urbanistiques of the exact parcel and read the lotissement's cahier des charges. These two documents override every figure on this page.

Height and levels

  • R+2 is capped at 11.50 m in most communal règlements (some allow 12 m), superstructures included.
  • Ground floor at 3.20 m and upper floors at 3.00 m floor-to-floor give a 2.80 m clear ceiling, the legal minimum inland (2.60 m within 25 km of the coast), and keep the first balcony at least 3.00 m above the sidewalk.
  • Roof: terrace slab at 9.20 m, parapet 1.10–1.20 m, stair bulkhead of at most 2.30 m, so the whole building stays under the 11.50 m "petit projet" line. Many règlements want that bulkhead 3 m back from the façade; this design puts the stair on the façade and depends on the commune accepting it.

The courtyard rule

  • Built footprint is capped around 84% of the lot. The remaining 16 m² must be an open-air courtyard through all levels.
  • The courtyard needs a clear side of at least 4.0 m ("vue directe") when bedrooms or the kitchen take their light from it.
  • Every habitable room must open on the street or on that courtyard. Party walls stay blind. A small courette may only serve bathrooms.

Rooms and stairs

  • Minimums by decree: main room 12 m², other bedrooms 9 m², kitchen 5 m², bathroom 1.30 m², room width 2.35 m, windows ≥ 1/10 of the floor area.
  • Shared stair: flights at least 1.00 m clear (Casablanca applies this to every main stair), 17 risers of 17.6 cm per 3.00 m level and 18 of 17.8 cm for the 3.20 m ground floor.
  • Balconies over the street only where the street is at least 10–12 m wide, with a projection capped at about 1/10 of its width.
Two things to verify before the architect starts

Whether the commune waives the on-plot parking space for lots under 160 m² (most do inside an authorised lotissement, otherwise a garage eats 15 m² of the ground floor), and whether the street in front is wide enough for balconies. If it is not, the plan keeps French windows with a railing and the outdoor space moves to the roof.

02 · Who buys, and why this sells

Design for the buyer who exists

The Moroccan urban household is 3.7 people, 61% of them own their home, and two thirds live in exactly this building type: the "maison marocaine moderne" of R+1 to R+2. A three-bedroom apartment is the natural product for a young family leaving rent, or for a Moroccan living abroad buying a summer and retirement base. Two facts from the 2025 market data shape everything else.

Three-bedroom units are slightly over-supplied

Two-bedroom apartments took 47% of purchase demand in 2025, and the last published split (early 2024) had three-bedroom units at about 31% of demand against 36% of supply. An F4 therefore does not sell on its bedroom count. It sells on light, on a salon that hosts twelve guests, on finishes that look above its price, and on a price the buyer can finance. Its real size is a strength: about 60 m² net, 71 m² built plus balcony, courtyard or terrace, which sits in the 50–80 m² band that 41% of buyers search.

Price is the first filter, aid is the second

Three out of four buyers rank price first. A new home sold at or under 700,000 MAD TTC to a first-time buyer can carry 70,000 MAD of direct state aid (Daam Sakane, 2024–2028), used by more than 105,000 households by June 2026, a quarter of them Moroccans living abroad. The aid is paid only when the seller is registered on the programme's platform, which a private individual may not be able to do; the legal section explains the choice. Where it applies, a price under that line widens the pool of buyers who can finance the purchase.

03 · The plan

One shell, two ways to divide it

The building is organised in three bands. On the street: the shared stair and the two best rooms, the salon marocain and a bedroom, both opening on the balcony. In the middle: a band glazed onto the courtyard that holds the kitchen and the bathroom. At the back: two rooms on either side of the 16.8 m² courtyard (16 m² is the minimum), which brings daylight to every room of the plan. The stair is a single straight flight along the party wall with a return gallery, so each apartment door opens at the back of the cage, away from the street, and the ground-floor lobby stays under a full-height slab. The same concrete frame carries both versions below.

What the geometry allows, honestly

With a 16 m² courtyard, a 1.10 m shared stair and the decree minimums, a 10 × 10 m lot leaves about 60 m² of net floor per apartment. Three bedrooms fit only if the kitchen is an open galley along the courtyard that also serves as the passage, the single bathroom is ventilated by duct, and there is no separate guest WC. That is Plan A, which answers the brief. Plan B trades the third bedroom for a closed kitchen, a real dining hall with a table for six and a larger bathroom; it is the version most Moroccan families would choose to live in. The three verifiers who checked both plans still expect Plan A to sell faster in a mid-market lotissement, because buyers count bedrooms first and the open kitchen can be screened with a glazed partition. Where the commune accepts a smaller courtyard (check the règlement and the lotissement's cahier des charges), every room in Plan A grows. Decide after reading the note de renseignements.

Plan A · three bedrooms, typical floor (1st and 2nd)

How Plan A lives

  • Arrival. The straight flight lands at the back of the cage on a 1.10 m landing; the apartment door opens from there into the kitchen band, with the parents' room to the left and the salon straight ahead.
  • Salon marocain, 4.0 × 3.4 m clear. A U of sedari 70 cm deep on three walls leaves 2.6 × 2.7 m of free floor for the tables; twelve guests sit comfortably. French window and balcony on the street. Daily meals take the place of one sedari side if the family prefers a table here.
  • Kitchen, 10.1 m². A 3.6 m counter along the salon wall, fridge at the end, a 3.4 m glass wall onto the courtyard and a small breakfast table. It is also the passage to the salon and the bedrooms, so it must be kept tidy; a black-framed glazed screen behind the counter hides the cooking from the salon door.
  • Night side. The parents' room and the third bedroom face the courtyard, quiet and private; the second bedroom faces the street with its own balcony access. Every bedroom has a built-in wardrobe.
  • Bathroom. One family bathroom of 1.6 × 1.8 m with a 90 × 90 shower, wall-hung WC and vanity, on a plumbing stack shared by the three floors, with a ventilation duct rising to the roof.

Compromises to state to your architect

  • The kitchen is an open galley that doubles as the corridor. Family buyers prefer closed kitchens; the glazed screen answers some of them, Plan B answers all of them.
  • There is one bathroom and no separate guest WC; it has no window, so it needs a duct with mechanical extraction. Some commissions ask for a courette instead; check. In either plan, only the ground-floor apartment can add a second WC, in its private courtyard, if the commune accepts it.
  • Chambre 3 is 2.4 m wide: a 140 bed along the wall and a 1.4 m wardrobe. It suits a child or a guest, not a couple.
  • The stair cage stands on the façade and its roof bulkhead comes close to the street line. If the règlement requires the bulkhead 3 m back, the stair moves to the back of the lot and the ground floor gets an entrance corridor; that costs the ground-floor apartment about 4 m².
  • The balcony assumes a street at least 10 m wide. On a narrower street, keep the French windows with a railing and the wall line stays flat.

Ground floor and roof

Plan B · two bedrooms, closed kitchen, dining hall

Plan B keeps the shell, the stair, the courtyard and the plumbing stacks of Plan A. It moves the kitchen into the courtyard-side room, closed and fitted, and deepens the middle band into the dining hall Moroccan families use daily, with a table for six and a sofa, while the salon marocain stays for guests. In the listings this reads as "salon marocain + salon européen", the formula that draws the most enquiries. Building Plan A on one floor and Plan B on another changes only partitions, not the frame.

04 · Style and finishes

Contemporary Moroccan, spent where the eye lands

The brief is elegant, not expensive. The rule below is simple: a calm, light shell everywhere, and Moroccan craft concentrated in the four places a visitor photographs. Buyers read those four places as "haut standing" and forgive a modest budget elsewhere.

Limewash whiteWalls, ceilings. Warm, not cream.
Tadelakt greyBathroom walls, one salon wall.
Bleu de FèsZellige accents, entrance door.
Courtyard greenZellige in the patio, planting.
CedarDoors, sedari frames, kitchen screen.
BrassHandles, taps, lamps. Small doses.

Spend here

  • Entrance and stair. Bejmat or terrazzo treads, a plastered gebs frieze at each landing, brass numbers, interphone and a solid wood or steel street door. The stair is the first thing every visitor sees and the only part all three buyers share.
  • Salon marocain. A carved plaster cornice, one wall in grey tadelakt behind the sedari, a cedar-framed arch to the dining zone, large-format porcelain tiles (60 × 120) or local marble.
  • Kitchen. Fitted units in matte lacquer, quartz or granite worktop, zellige splashback in one colour, the trio (hob, hood, oven) installed, and in Plan A a black-framed glazed partition that closes it off from the dining side. Buyers price a fitted kitchen far above its cost.
  • Bathrooms. Italian shower with a linear drain, tadelakt or large-format tiles, a wall-hung WC, black or brass fittings, a mirror with a light. One good bathroom sells more than two average ones.

Keep simple here

  • Bedrooms. Wood-look laminate or porcelain, built-in wardrobes with plain doors, one pendant. Storage is what buyers notice.
  • Ceilings. A full dropped ceiling with spots only fits in coastal cities, where 2.60 m is legal; inland keep the 2.80 m and light the salon from a perimeter cove behind the cornice.
  • Joinery. Aluminium double glazing in a dark bronze or anthracite finish, sliding where space is tight. The colour is what makes an ordinary window look considered. Casablanca is preparing a façade rule that imposes light colours and silver aluminium; check it before ordering.
  • Façade. Smooth white render with a stone or brick plinth to 60 cm, and a metal claustra with a Moroccan geometric motif on the balcony fronts. It gives privacy, shade, and a signature that shows in the listing photo.
  • Courtyard. Zellige or terracotta on the floor, one climbing plant, a wall light. It becomes the second photo of the listing.

05 · Budget and pricing

Where the margin comes from

The building costs roughly the same everywhere in Morocco. The land and the selling price do not. In 2026 a moyen-standing R+2 of about 280 m² covered costs 1.3 to 1.8 million MAD to build before land, studies and contingency. Whether the three apartments then sell at 6,000 or 13,000 MAD per m² depends on the quartier, and that single number decides the project. Use the calculator with prices from sold comparables in the exact neighbourhood, not portal asking prices, which run two to three times above real transactions in the mid market.

Three price levels in one building

Promoters price the floors differently, and so should you. Ground floors trade 10–20% under the middle floor unless they get something the others lack; top floors with a private terrace trade 15–25% above. In this design the ground floor gets the private courtyard and the top floor gets the roof terrace, which narrows the gap and lifts the average.

  • Ground floor: middle-floor price minus 8%. Sell it as "appartement avec cour privative", never as "RDC".
  • First floor: the reference price. Make it the furnished show apartment, since it is also the easiest to visit.
  • Second floor: plus 10–12% for the terrace, the light and the absence of a neighbour above.

If the middle-floor price lands just above 700,000 MAD TTC, price the ground floor under the line: it turns the hardest unit to sell into the one that comes with 70,000 MAD of state aid.

06 · From land to keys

The path that ends with three separate titles

Selling three apartments in one building means the building must become a copropriété with one titre foncier per lot before the first deed is signed. Most delays in small Moroccan projects come from starting that paperwork after the building is finished. Start it with the permit.

  1. Month 0Check the lot before paying for it. Obtain the note de renseignements urbanistiques (Agence Urbaine, 2 working days), read the lotissement cahier des charges, confirm the titre foncier is clean at the ANCFCC (Mohafadati), and confirm R+2, the courtyard rule, the balcony rule and the parking waiver in writing.
  2. Months 1–3Design and permit. Sign an architect registered with the Ordre (mandatory in urban communes) and a bureau d'études for the reinforced-concrete file under RPS 2011. The architect files on Rokhas.ma: plans, structural note, RTCM thermal sheet, title, cadastral plan. An R+2 under 11.50 m is a "petit projet": 15 days statutory, 2–4 months in practice. Pay the communal construction tax (10–30 MAD per m² covered) and ask the architect to keep the whole building, stair bulkhead included, under 11.50 m so it stays a "petit projet".
  3. Month 3Soil study and insurance. Order the étude de sol before foundations (about 6,000–15,000 MAD). Since 30 December 2024 professional builders must carry all-risks site and ten-year liability cover; ask your contractor for both even if the threshold spares a small R+2.
  4. Months 4–16Build. Twelve to fifteen months is realistic for structure, second fix and finishes on three apartments; Moroccan guides quote 12–18 for an R+2. Post the site board, keep the site log (loi 66-12), and never enclose the courtyard or add a level: the commune inspects against the approved plans and an infraction blocks the permis d'habiter.
  5. Months 10–14Prepare the copropriété while walls go up. A surveyor (IGT) draws the division plan and the règlement de copropriété under loi 18-00 from the approved plans. Have the notaire review both. Individual water and electricity meters per apartment are requested from the distributor now.
  6. Months 16–19Permis d'habiter. File the déclaration d'achèvement with the architect's attestation of conformity. Thirty days by law, one to six months in practice depending on the commune. Without it there is no notarial sale, no bank loan and no Daam Sakane eligibility.
  7. Months 17–21Split the title. The Conservation Foncière registers the règlement de copropriété and creates one titre foncier per apartment with its share of common parts. Only then can a buyer's bank register a mortgage on "their" apartment.
  8. Months 15–22Sell. Marketing can start earlier (see the sales plan), but reservation contracts signed before completion fall under the off-plan law (VEFA, loi 107-12): a notarised preliminary contract, a bank guarantee for deposits and staged payments. Many small builders wait for the permis d'habiter and sell finished, which is simpler and what most first-time buyers prefer.
07 · Selling fast

A plan to sell three apartments in one season

A well-priced new apartment in Morocco sells in two to six months; an over-priced one sits for a year or more, and the national average is about eight months. Speed comes from four decisions made before the first listing goes online: the price, the paperwork, the show apartment, and the calendar.

1 · Price from sold comparables, then under the aid line

Take five to ten recent sales of new three-bedroom apartments within a kilometre, from a notaire, a bank appraiser or the Yakeey and Agenz referentials, and price the first floor at their median per m². Do not price from Mubawab asking prices. Where the result lands within 10% of 700,000 MAD TTC, pull the ground floor under it so that unit carries the 70,000 MAD Daam Sakane aid. A price that a bank's appraiser confirms is a price that closes.

2 · Paperwork before marketing

List only when you can hand a buyer three documents on the first visit: the permis d'habiter, the individual titre foncier of the apartment, and a bank pre-agreement letter from one or two banks that have already appraised the building. Buyers who can borrow sign; buyers who must wait for your paperwork keep visiting other projects.

3 · One furnished show apartment

Furnish the first floor: the salon marocain with sedari and a lantern, the dining table, the master bedroom, plants in the courtyard. Budget 30,000–50,000 MAD, sold with the unit or moved to the next. Photograph it at dusk with lights on, shoot a 60-second vertical walkthrough, and put both on every listing. Visits convert on the salon and the bathroom, so those two must be finished to the last detail.

4 · Launch in spring, close in summer

Purchase demand peaks in April–May and again in August, when 4.6 million Moroccans living abroad visit. Plan the permis d'habiter for February, list right after Eid al-Fitr (Ramadan 2027 ends around 9 March), and hold weekend open days in July and August for MRE families. Avoid a first launch between October and January.

Two decisions to settle with a notaire before buying the land

  • Promoter or private seller. The state aid is paid only when the seller is registered as a promoter on the Daam Sakane platform; sales by private individuals are described as excluded. Registering, possibly through a small company, opens the aid to your buyers but brings VAT and professional income tax on the operation. Selling as a private individual keeps the property-profit tax (20% of the gain, minimum 3% of the price) but forfeits the aid argument and still risks requalification as a developer. Get a written answer for your case.
  • Sell finished or off-plan. Off-plan sales fall under the VEFA law and need a bank guarantee that individuals rarely obtain, so deposits stay locked with the notaire. For three units, sell finished, but collect names from the day the structure is up. A 2024 reform requires promises of sale on titled property to be notarised, so never take a cash deposit against a private paper: route every reservation through the notaire.
